      How to use it system-wide
      • Windows: install Equalizer APO and paste the APO config into its config.txt (or Import it in Peace). It is the only export that carries crossover, delay and polarity.
      • Android: in Wavelet, open Graphic equalizer, then Import the GraphicEQ file.

      Pro missions

      Two real jobs. Your ears on the line.

      Mission 01 · Live sound

      An in-ear mix for a blast-beat drummer

      Soundcheck, ten minutes to doors. The drummer's in-ear monitors are flat, and the overhead mics are drowning everything in cymbal wash. They want a kick they can feel, a snare that cracks and no boxy toms. Shape their mix with four bands.

      • Kick thump: a low shelf at 70 Hz
      • Boxiness: a cut around 380 Hz
      • Snare crack: a lift at 4 kHz
      • Cymbal wash: a cut at 9 kHz

      Mission 02 · Desk studio

      Blend a subwoofer with bookshelf speakers

      Small powered speakers roll off around 65 Hz, so you add an 8-inch sub under the desk, 75 cm further away than the speakers. Right now it booms in one place and vanishes in another. Make the solid line flat from 35 to 200 Hz.

      • Pick a crossover frequency
      • Match the sub's level
      • Line up the timing with phase and polarity
      • Two different setups can work. Find one.

          1. The range: 20 Hz to 20 kHz

          Each octave doubles the frequency: 20, 40, 80, 160, 320, 640 Hz, then 1.3, 2.6, 5.1, 10 and 20 kHz. That makes 10 octaves, and each one gets equal space on an EQ because your ear hears pitch logarithmically.

          Young ears reach about 20 kHz. Most adults over 25 stop around 15 to 17 kHz, so the top octave matters less than its size suggests. The most sensitive zone is 2 to 5 kHz, where the ear canal resonates. Small changes there are very audible.

          3. How many bands? The common layouts

          A graphic EQ has fixed bands you only raise or lower. More bands means finer control. Tap a band to hear it.

          5 bands: Android's built-in equalizer

          6 bands: Spotify's equalizer

          10 bands: one per octave (ISO standard, most DSPs and apps)

          31 bands: one per third-octave (studio and live sound)

          20 · 25 · 31.5 · 40 · 50 · 63 · 80 · 100 · 125 · 160 · 200 · 250 · 315 · 400 · 500 · 630 · 800 · 1k · 1.25k · 1.6k · 2k · 2.5k · 3.15k · 4k · 5k · 6.3k · 8k · 10k · 12.5k · 16k · 20k

          A parametric EQ has no fixed bands: you place each filter anywhere. Five to ten parametric filters usually beat 31 graphic bands, which is why AutoEq, Equalizer APO, Qudelix and most DSPs use them.

          4. Parametric EQ: frequency, gain, Q

          Frequency (Fc)
          Where the filter acts, in Hz.
          Gain
          How much it boosts (+) or cuts (−), in dB. +6 dB is roughly twice the acoustic pressure, and sounds clearly louder.
          Q
          How wide the filter is. Higher Q is narrower. Q 0.7 spans about 2 octaves (broad, musical); Q 1.4 is 1 octave; Q 4.3 is a third of an octave; Q 6 or more is surgical, for one resonance.
          Filter typeShorthandWhat it doesTypical use
          Peak (bell)PKRaises or lowers a bump around FcFix a peak, add presence
          Low shelfLSC / LSMoves everything below FcBass to taste (around 105 Hz, Q 0.7)
          High shelfHSC / HSMoves everything above FcTreble or air (around 10 kHz)
          High-passHPFRemoves what is below FcProtect small speakers from deep bass
          Low-passLPFRemoves what is above FcSubwoofer crossover

          5. Preamp: why every boost needs a cut

          Digital audio cannot go above 0 dBFS. If a song already peaks near 0 and you boost 6 dB of bass, it clips and crackles. The fix is a preamp equal to your largest boost: +6 dB peak means −6 dB preamp. The Tuning Lab computes this for you.

          The cut makes things a bit quieter, so turn the volume up. It's also why cutting what's too loud usually beats boosting what's too quiet.

          6. Step by step

          1. Fix the fit first. An IEM tip one size too small can lose 10 dB of bass. No EQ fixes a bad seal.
          2. Fix your volume. Louder always sounds "better", so keep it the same while comparing.
          3. Load a measured correction for your exact model (Tuning Lab, step 1). This brings it close to a research-backed target.
          4. Set the preamp to the largest boost.
          5. Bass to taste with a low shelf at about 105 Hz, in 1 dB steps.
          6. Treble to taste with a high shelf at about 10 kHz, or tame one harsh peak with a narrow cut (Q 3 to 6) between 5 and 9 kHz.
          7. Fix specific problems: mud at 200–400 Hz, shout at 2–4 kHz, sibilance at 5–9 kHz.
          8. Compare level-matched with the EQ switch in the dock, and live with it for a day before changing more.
          9. Save presets per device, and per genre if you like.

          7. Tuning by device

          IEMs

          Seal is everything; try every tip size. Peaks between 6 and 10 kHz differ from ear to ear, so use narrow cuts and trust your ears over the graph. Sensitive IEMs can hiss, so lower the source gain.

          Earbuds and TWS (AirPods, Galaxy Buds)

          They already run their own DSP; AirPods use Adaptive EQ. ANC on, off, and transparency each sound different, so use the matching profile (AutoEq has several for AirPods Pro 2).

          Over-ear headphones

          Worn pads and glasses leak bass. Open-backs roll off in the sub-bass; boost it gently, because large boosts push the driver into distortion.

          Desk speakers

          Placement first: an equilateral triangle, tweeters at ear height, away from walls. Below about 300 Hz the room dominates. Cut room peaks; never boost dips, they won't fill. Measure with a microphone and REW.

          Hardware DSPs and dongles

          Qudelix 5K, FiiO BTR/K-series and miniDSP store a parametric EQ on the device, so it follows you to any phone or PC. Enter the same filters the Lab exports.

          Targets

          Harman over-ear (2018) and Harman in-ear (2019) are the research-based "most people prefer this" curves. Diffuse field is flatter and more neutral. AutoEq defaults to Harman.

          9. Common mistakes

          • The "smile" curve: big bass and treble boosts with scooped mids. Exciting for a minute, fatiguing after an hour.
          • Boosting more than 6 dB anywhere. It adds distortion and needs a big preamp cut.
          • Skipping the preamp, which gives quiet crackles on loud songs.
          • A/B tests at different volumes. The louder side always wins.
          • EQing around a bad fit instead of changing tips or pads.
          • Chasing the graph above 8 kHz with IEMs, where measurements and real ears differ most.
